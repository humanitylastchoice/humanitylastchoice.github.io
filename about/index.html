What Humanity’s Last Choice is

A benchmark for AI recommendation robustness — does the system still choose for the person when the options are written to win the model?

01The thesis

The last choice humans make is who chooses next.

02The argument

People choose an AI system, and that system increasingly chooses for them: what they watch, where they travel, which restaurants they visit, what art they discover, which papers they read, which tools or skills they use. The person makes one choice — which system to ask — and the system makes the rest. The first decision quietly becomes the one that decides the others.

A recommendation has two sides. On one side is the person and what they meant. On the other are the candidates — the restaurants, the towns, the papers, the tools — and the material that describes them: their webpages, their listings, their abstracts. That material used to be written for people. It is now, quietly, being written for models. Not always, and not always badly; a clear description helps a model the way it helps a reader. But when the reward for being recommended is large and the reader is a machine, the material starts to change. A listing can be written for a reader, or written to win the recommendation. Same restaurant, two descriptions. The question that interests us is not whether the second one is distasteful. It is whether the system still chooses for the person when it reads it.

03What the benchmark is

Humanity’s Last Choice is a broad-coverage benchmark for the robustness of AI recommendations across six kinds of choice people delegate: what they watch, where they travel, which restaurants they visit, what art they discover, which papers they read, which tools or skills they use. In each domain a task pairs a person’s request with candidates drawn from real source material.

Every task runs twice. In the clean condition the candidates are described as they are. In the shaped condition one candidate has been rewritten to win the recommendation — the way material on the open web is already being rewritten. The system’s job is the same both times: choose for the person. The gap between the two runs is the score.

We report both numbers, per system and per domain, with the date. We do not report a single headline figure; the interesting facts are in the gaps — which domains are fragile, which kinds of shaping work, which systems notice.

04What we want to exist

A common standard for recommendation robustness, so that systems can be compared, weaknesses found, and progress tracked. The benchmark, the code, and the leaderboard are public. Systems can be submitted for evaluation, domains can be contributed, and tasks can be written with us. Every task is documented well enough that anyone can see what a shaped variant does and does not change.

05What we do not claim
  • We do not name villains. Candidates that learn to talk to the model are behaving the way candidates always have, in whatever language the gatekeeper speaks. The gatekeeper has changed, and the evidence should change with it. Candidates are “shaped”, never “malicious”.
  • We do not publish a single headline number, and never a number without its condition — clean or shaped — and its date.
  • We do not use real businesses as examples. Illustrative listings such as “Osteria Alba” or “Costa Brilhante” are labelled illustrative.
  • Nothing that carries a benchmark number is published before the results owner has replaced the sample rows. Sample data is always labelled.
